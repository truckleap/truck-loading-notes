---
title: "How Many Tie-Downs Does a Flatbed Load Need?"
description: "A plain-language walk through the federal minimum tie-down count and working load limits."
---

Securement rules for open-deck freight come from the federal cargo securement regulations (49 CFR 393, Subpart I). This page covers the two numbers drivers check most often: how many tie-downs an article needs, and how much working load limit they need to add up to. Commodity-specific rules (steel coils, lumber, machinery, vehicles and others) add their own requirements on top of these, so always check the section for your freight.

## Minimum number of tie-downs (393.110)

For an article that is not blocked against forward movement by a headboard, bulkhead or other cargo:

| Article | Minimum tie-downs |
|---|---|
| 5 ft long or less and 1,100 lb or less | 1 |
| 5 ft long or less but over 1,100 lb | 2 |
| Over 5 ft up to 10 ft, any weight | 2 |
| Over 10 ft | 2 for the first 10 ft, plus 1 for each additional 10 ft or part of it |

Example: a 24-foot bundle needs 2 for the first 10 feet, plus 1 for feet 11 to 20, plus 1 for feet 21 to 24. That is 4 tie-downs.

## Working load limit (393.106)

The working load limits of all tie-downs on an article must add up to at least half the article's weight. A 30,000 lb load needs at least 15,000 lb of aggregate WLL. Use the WLL marked on the strap or chain, not the breaking strength. Unmarked straps get a low default value, so a pile of unlabeled straps can leave you short.

Direct tie-downs (attached to the cargo and the trailer) count at 50% of their WLL. Indirect tie-downs (over the top, trailer to trailer) count at full WLL.

## A quick pre-trip check

1. Count articles and measure the longest one.
2. Work out the tie-down count from the table.
3. Add up the marked WLL and compare it to half the weight.
4. Recheck within the first 50 miles and at every duty change.

Knowing the count before you accept also tells you how long securement and tarping will take, which belongs in the rate.
